\subsection{}\label{q1a}
\task{Do we have Complete Markets in this economy?}
Yes.

\textbf{Definition (Complete Markets).} Markets are complete if there is one contingent claim
(Arrow-Debreu security) for each state of nature $n = 1, \dots, N$, where the contingent claim for
state $n$ pays \$1 if state $n$ materializes and \$0 otherwise (Module 2, slide 1.3).

Let's define the payoff matrix
\[
  \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 20 & 30 \\ 10 & 10 & 10 \\ 0 & 10 & 10 \end{bmatrix}
\]
(rows: assets $A$, $B$, $C$; columns: states $n = 1, 2, 3$). Then there exists $\bm{\Omega}$ such
that $\mathbf{X}'\bm{\Omega} = \mathbf{I}$, with $\bm{\Omega} = (\mathbf{X}')^{-1}$, if $\mathbf{X}'$ is
invertible.

Even if a contingent claim is not traded directly, it exists in this economy if a portfolio of the traded
assets $A$, $B$, and $C$ replicates it, i.e., pays \$1 in state $n$ and \$0 otherwise. $\bm{\Omega}$ tells us
how many units of each asset to hold to replicate the contingent claims (column $n$
of $\bm{\Omega}$ is the replicating portfolio for the contingent claim of state $n$). Since
$\mathbf{X}'\bm{\Omega} = \mathbf{I}$, if $\mathbf{X}'$ is invertible, then a replicating portfolio for
each contingent claim exists in this economy.

Therefore, checking whether $\mathbf{X}'$ is invertible is sufficient for Complete Markets in this
economy. Since $\det(\mathbf{X}') = 1000 \neq 0$, $\mathbf{X}'$ is invertible. Therefore, this market is
complete.

\subsection{}\label{q1b}
\task{Suppose that the Law of One Price (LOP) holds in this economy. Calculate the price of each Contingent Claim (or Arrow-Debreu security) in this economy. [Footnote 2: [...] You need to use the LOP to solve for $\mathbf{P}_c$ as a function of $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{P}$.]}
\paragraph{Setup.}
Let $P_c(n)$ denote the price of the contingent claim for state $n$. Then
\begin{align*}
  P(A) &= \sum_{n=1}^{3} P_c(n)\,\mathrm{Payoff}_A(n), \\
  P(B) &= \sum_{n=1}^{3} P_c(n)\,\mathrm{Payoff}_B(n), \\
  P(C) &= \sum_{n=1}^{3} P_c(n)\,\mathrm{Payoff}_C(n).
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Method.}
Or, in matrix form,
\[
  \mathbf{P} = \mathbf{X} \mathbf{P}_c,
\]
where $\mathbf{X}$ is the payoff matrix from Question~\ref{q1a},
\[
  \mathbf{P}_c = \big(P_c(1), P_c(2), P_c(3)\big)', \quad\text{and}\quad
  \mathbf{P} = \big(P(A), P(B), P(C)\big)' = (16, 8, 6)'.
\]

\paragraph{Result.}
We confirmed in Question~\ref{q1a} that $\mathbf{X}$ is invertible. So, we can calculate
$\mathbf{P}_c = \mathbf{X}^{-1}\mathbf{P}$. Therefore, the prices of the Contingent Claims are
\[
  \mathbf{P}_c = \mathbf{X}^{-1}\mathbf{P}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0.1 & -0.1 \\ -0.1 & 0.1 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & -0.1 & -0.1 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 16 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 \\ 0.4 \\ 0.2 \end{bmatrix},
\]
i.e., $P_c(1) = 0.2$, $P_c(2) = 0.4$, and $P_c(3) = 0.2$, where
\[
  \mathbf{X}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0.1 & -0.1 \\ -0.1 & 0.1 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & -0.1 & -0.1 \end{bmatrix}
  \qquad (\det(\mathbf{X}) = 1000)
\]
(rows: states $n = 1, 2, 3$; columns: assets $A$, $B$, $C$).

\subsection{}\label{q1c}
\task{Suppose that the LOP holds in this economy and introduce a new asset (referred to as asset $D$) with payoff vector $\mathrm{Payoff}(D) = (30, 20, 10)$ for our three states of nature $n = (1, 2, 3)$. Calculate the price of asset $D$ in this economy}
\paragraph{Method.}
As calculated in Question~\ref{q1b}, $\mathbf{P}_c = (0.2, 0.4, 0.2)'$. Taking the inner product of
$\mathbf{Payoff}(D) = (30, 20, 10)'$ with $\mathbf{P}_c$ gives the price of asset $D$ in this economy:

\paragraph{Result.}
\begin{align*}
  P(D) &= \mathbf{Payoff}(D)'\,\mathbf{P}_c
        = \begin{bmatrix} 30 & 20 & 10 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 \\ 0.4 \\ 0.2 \end{bmatrix} \\
       &= 30 \cdot 0.2 + 20 \cdot 0.4 + 10 \cdot 0.2 = 16.
\end{align*}

\subsection{}\label{q1d}
\task{(1) Calculate the Stochastic Discount Factor (SDF) realization under each of the three states of nature in this economy.\par (2) Does this economy satisfy No-Arbitrage (explain)?\par (3) Is there anything interesting about the SDF in this economy (if so, explain what it means in economy terms)?}
\paragraph{Setup.}
The SDF $M$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}[M \cdot \mathrm{Payoff}] = \mathrm{Price}$ for every asset in
this economy:
\begin{align*}
  P(A) &= \sum_{n=1}^{3} \pi(n)\,M(n)\,\mathrm{Payoff}_A(n), \\
  P(B) &= \sum_{n=1}^{3} \pi(n)\,M(n)\,\mathrm{Payoff}_B(n), \\
  P(C) &= \sum_{n=1}^{3} \pi(n)\,M(n)\,\mathrm{Payoff}_C(n).
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Method.}
Or, in matrix form,
\[
  \mathbf{P} = \mathbf{X}_\pi \mathbf{M}, \qquad
  \mathbf{X}_\pi = \begin{bmatrix}
    10 \cdot \frac{1}{4} & 20 \cdot \frac{1}{2} & 30 \cdot \frac{1}{4} \\[2pt]
    10 \cdot \frac{1}{4} & 10 \cdot \frac{1}{2} & 10 \cdot \frac{1}{4} \\[2pt]
    0 & 10 \cdot \frac{1}{2} & 10 \cdot \frac{1}{4}
  \end{bmatrix}
\]
(rows: assets $A$, $B$, $C$; columns: states $n = 1, 2, 3$; $\mathbf{X}$ with column $n$ multiplied by
$\pi(n)$), where $\mathbf{M} = \big(M(1), M(2), M(3)\big)'$ and $\mathbf{P} = \big(P(A), P(B), P(C)\big)'$.

\paragraph{Result (1).}
Since $\det(\mathbf{X}_\pi) = 31.25 \neq 0$, $\mathbf{X}_\pi$ is invertible, with
\[
  \mathbf{X}_\pi^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0.4 & -0.4 \\ -0.2 & 0.2 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & -0.4 & -0.4 \end{bmatrix}
\]
(rows: states $n = 1, 2, 3$; columns: assets $A$, $B$, $C$). Therefore, the realizations of the SDF are
\[
  \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{X}_\pi^{-1}\mathbf{P}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0.4 & -0.4 \\ -0.2 & 0.2 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & -0.4 & -0.4 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 16 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 \\ 0.8 \\ 0.8 \end{bmatrix},
\]
i.e., $M(1) = M(2) = M(3) = 0.8$.

\paragraph{Interpretation (2).}
This economy satisfies No-Arbitrage.

\textbf{Definition (No-Arbitrage).} (Module 2, slide 1.3)
\begin{itemize}
  \item An asset with $\mathrm{Payoff} \geq 0$ must have $\mathrm{Price} \geq 0$.
  \item An asset with $\mathrm{Payoff} \geq 0$ and a chance for $\mathrm{Payoff} > 0$ must have
    $\mathrm{Price} > 0$.
\end{itemize}

Each Arrow-Debreu contingent claim has $\mathrm{Payoff} \geq 0$ with a chance for $\mathrm{Payoff} > 0$,
and from Question~\ref{q1b} its price is positive ($P_c(1) = 0.2$, $P_c(2) = 0.4$, $P_c(3) = 0.2$). The
contingent claims therefore satisfy the No-Arbitrage condition above. Moreover, with complete markets and the
LOP, the price of any payoff is determined by
\[
  P = \sum_{n=1}^{3} P_c(n)\,\mathrm{Payoff}(n)
\]
(the formula used in Questions~\ref{q1b} and~\ref{q1c}). Since $P_c(n) > 0$ for all $n$, any asset with
$\mathrm{Payoff} \geq 0$ has $\mathrm{Price} \geq 0$, and if its payoff is positive in at least one state,
its price is positive. Hence No-Arbitrage holds in this economy.

\paragraph{Interpretation (3).}
As we can see, the realized SDF is the same in every state of the economy ($M(n) = 0.8$ for all $n$). Since
$M$ is constant across states, its covariance with every asset's return is zero,
$\mathrm{Cov}(M, R_j) = 0$. That is, this is an economy in which the expected excess return of every asset is
zero (no risk premium).

\subsection{}\label{q1e}
\task{Show that the price that the SDF assigns to asset $D$ in this economy is the same as the price that you calculated in Question (1c)}
\paragraph{Method.}
Using the SDF from Question~\ref{q1d} ($M(n) = 0.8$ for all $n$), with $\mathrm{Payoff}_D(1) = 30$,
$\mathrm{Payoff}_D(2) = 20$, and $\mathrm{Payoff}_D(3) = 10$,

\paragraph{Result.}
\begin{align*}
  P(D) &= \sum_{n=1}^{3} \pi(n)\,M(n)\,\mathrm{Payoff}_D(n) \\
       &= 0.25 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 30 + 0.5 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 20 + 0.25 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 10 \\
       &= 16,
\end{align*}
which is the same as the price of asset $D$ calculated in Question~\ref{q1c}.

\subsection{}\label{q2a}
\task{(1) Find the SDF $M^*_t = a - \mathbf{b}'\mathbf{xR}_t = a - xR^*_t$ that unconditionally prices the excess returns of the 10 portfolios in the BM Dataset (ex-post). That is, find the SDF that satisfies $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[M^* \cdot \mathbf{xR}] = \mathbf{0}$ for the $\mathbf{xR}$ vector in the BM Dataset. [Footnote 3: Let's use the normalization $\mathbb{E}[M^*] = 1$ (throughout Question 2). [...]]\par (2) Provide a scatterplot with the portfolio deciles (1 to 10) in the x-axis and the respective $b$ coefficients in the y-axis.\par (3) Describe the pattern you observe in the scatterplot and explain why this pattern emerges.}
\paragraph{Setup.}
The SDF $M^*_t = a - \mathbf{b}'\mathbf{xR}_t$ prices the excess returns $\mathbf{xR}_t$ of the 10
value-weighted book-to-market decile portfolios (``Lo 10'' to ``Hi 10'', Ken French data library), in
excess of the Fama--French risk-free rate. Data: monthly returns (one-month horizon), July 1927 to August
2026 ($T = \QtwocT$ months), in decimals.

\paragraph{Method.}
With the normalization $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[M^*] = 1$, $a$ and $\mathbf{b}$ solve the sample moment
conditions
\begin{align*}
  a - \mathbf{b}'\,\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{xR}] &= 1, \\
  a\,\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{xR}] - \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{xR}\,\mathbf{xR}']\,\mathbf{b} &= \mathbf{0},
\end{align*}
where $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot]$ is the time average.

\ph{[Derivation that the two moment conditions give $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = \hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{xR}]$
(with $\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}$ dividing by $T$): to be written by the student.]}

\paragraph{Result (1)--(2).}
Figure~\ref{fig:q2a_b} plots $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ by decile (estimates in \texttt{output/q2a\_sdf\_coefficients.csv}).

    excess returns of the 10 value-weighted book-to-market decile portfolios, under the normalization
    $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[M^*] = 1$. Data: monthly returns (one-month horizon), July 1927 to August 2026

\paragraph{Interpretation (3).}
% Student-written text from spec/q2a.md (typeset by AI, AI_INTERACTIONS.md).
The estimated coefficients $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ show no systematic pattern across the book-to-market deciles.
First, $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ is not monotonic in the decile: it neither increases nor decreases as the
book-to-market ratio rises. Second, adjacent deciles sometimes have coefficients of opposite signs (e.g.,
$\hat{b}_6 \approx 3.0$ and $\hat{b}_7 \approx -5.3$).

This pattern emerges from the closed form of $\mathbf{b}$ implied by the Method:
$\hat{\mathbf{b}} = \hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{xR}]$, where $\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}$ is the
covariance matrix of the 10 excess returns. Adjacent deciles are highly correlated (pairwise correlations of
about \QtwoaCorrMin{} to \QtwoaCorrMax{}). Inverting this nearly collinear $\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}$ and multiplying
by $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{xR}]$ can make the weights of adjacent deciles vary greatly, even with
opposite signs.

\paragraph{Interpretation (1), (3).}
% Student-written text from spec/q2b.md (typeset by AI, AI_INTERACTIONS.md).
The estimated coefficients $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ for the momentum deciles show no monotonic pattern. In the
middle deciles (2 to 9), as in Question~\ref{q2a}, there is no clear pattern: the coefficients change sign
without a systematic order (e.g., $\hat{b}_5 \approx 2.5$ and $\hat{b}_6 \approx -0.8$). The two extreme
deciles, however, resemble the familiar momentum pattern: the SDF takes a large short position in the loser
decile ($\hat{b}_1 \approx -4.0$) and a large long position in the winner decile
($\hat{b}_{10} \approx 3.4$), i.e., long past winners and short past losers.

In the middle deciles, the pattern emerges for the same reason as in Question~\ref{q2a}: adjacent deciles
are highly correlated (pairwise correlations of about \QtwobCorrMin{} to \QtwobCorrMax{}) and have similar
average excess returns, so inverting $\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}$ in
$\hat{\mathbf{b}} = \hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{xR}]$ can make the weights of adjacent
deciles vary greatly. At the extremes, however, the weights likely reflect the actual momentum premium. The
difference in average excess returns between decile 10 and decile 1 is large (about \QtwobSpread\% per
month), which is hard to attribute to noise and is better viewed as a real signal, and deciles 1 and 10 are
relatively less correlated (about \QtwobCorrExt). As a result, the weight on decile 10 is positive and the
weight on decile 1 is negative.

The correlation between the SDF from Question~\ref{q2a} and the SDF from Question~\ref{q2b} is positive
(about \QtwobCorr). Since a high SDF realization marks a high-marginal-utility (``bad'') state, a positive
correlation means that the two SDFs partly agree on which states are bad. However, the correlation is far from
1, which suggests that the two SDFs also capture different dimensions of risk.

Hansen--Jagannathan bound, $\mathrm{Vol}(M) \geq SR \cdot \mathbb{E}[M]$: since the maximum Sharpe ratio is
larger with 20 assets, the lower bound on the volatility of an SDF that prices all 20 assets is larger than
the bound for an SDF that prices only 10 of them.

\subsection{}\label{q3a}
\task{Using a power utility function, $U(W) = W^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$, with $\gamma$ representing the constant relative risk aversion, show that the SDF $M_t = R_{w,t}^{-\gamma}$ prices the vector of excess returns (i.e., show that $\mathbb{E}_t[R_{w,t+1}^{-\gamma} \cdot \mathbf{xR}_{t+1}] = \mathbf{0}$).}
% Typeset by AI from the student's solution in spec/q3a.md (formatting/transcription; AI_INTERACTIONS.md).
\paragraph{Method.}
From the maximization problem in Equation (3.2),
\[
  \bm{\varpi}^*_t = \arg\max_{\bm{\varpi}_t} \mathbb{E}_t\big[U(W_{t+1})\big]
  \quad \text{s.t.} \quad W_{t+1} = W_t \cdot \big(R_{f,t+1} + \bm{\varpi}_t' \mathbf{xR}_{t+1}\big),
\]
where $R_{w,t+1} = R_{f,t+1} + \bm{\varpi}^{*\prime}_t \mathbf{xR}_{t+1}$ is the return on the optimal
portfolio, so that $W_{t+1} = W_t \cdot R_{w,t+1}$.

The first-order condition is
\begin{align*}
  & \frac{\partial\, \mathbb{E}_t[U(W_{t+1})]}{\partial \bm{\varpi}_t}
    = \mathbb{E}_t\big[U'(W_{t+1}) \cdot W_t \cdot \mathbf{xR}_{t+1}\big] = \mathbf{0} \\
  \Longleftrightarrow\quad
  & \mathbb{E}_t\big[U'(W_{t+1}) \cdot \mathbf{xR}_{t+1}\big] = \mathbf{0}.
\end{align*}
Since $W_t$ is the investor's wealth at time $t$, it is positive ($W_t > 0$) and known at time $t$,
so it can be taken outside $\mathbb{E}_t[\cdot]$ and divided out.

Because $U'(W_{t+1}) = W_{t+1}^{-\gamma} = (W_t \cdot R_{w,t+1})^{-\gamma}$,
\begin{align*}
  & \mathbb{E}_t\big[(W_t \cdot R_{w,t+1})^{-\gamma} \cdot \mathbf{xR}_{t+1}\big] = \mathbf{0} \\
  \Longleftrightarrow\quad
  & W_t^{-\gamma}\, \mathbb{E}_t\big[R_{w,t+1}^{-\gamma} \cdot \mathbf{xR}_{t+1}\big] = \mathbf{0} \\
  \Longleftrightarrow\quad
  & \mathbb{E}_t\big[R_{w,t+1}^{-\gamma} \cdot \mathbf{xR}_{t+1}\big] = \mathbf{0},
\end{align*}
where the last step uses the same argument for $W_t^{-\gamma} > 0$.

\paragraph{Result.}
Therefore, $M_{t+1} = R_{w,t+1}^{-\gamma}$ prices the vector of excess returns. Because an SDF that
prices only excess returns still does so after multiplication by any positive factor (here,
$W_t^{-\gamma}$), the scale of $M$ is not identified; a normalization is therefore needed and is
innocuous.

\subsection{}\label{q4a}
\task{[...] (1) Show that we also have (as an approximation) a conditionally linear CAPM, $M_{t+1} \approx a_t - b_t \cdot R_{w,t+1}$, and provide expressions for $a_t$ and $b_t$. [...]\par (2) Highlight a disadvantage of the $M_{t+1} \approx a_t - b_t \cdot R_{w,t+1}$ relative to the original $M_{t+1} = R_{w,t+1}^{-\gamma}$ (beyond the fact that the linear SDF is an approximation).}
% Typeset by AI from the student's notes in spec/q4a.md (formatting/transcription; AI_INTERACTIONS.md).

\paragraph{(1) Conditionally linear CAPM.}
Let $M_{t+1} = M(F_{t+1}; \theta)$. It can be approximated by a Taylor expansion around
$\mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}]$:
\[
  M(F_{t+1}; \theta)
  = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{M^{(n)}\big(\mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}]; \theta\big)}{n!}
    \big(F_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}]\big)^n .
\]
Terms of second order and above can be neglected. Then,
\[
  M(F_{t+1}; \theta)
  \approx M\big(\mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}]; \theta\big)
  + M'\big(\mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}]; \theta\big)\big(F_{t+1} - \mathbb{E}_t[F_{t+1}]\big).
\]
If we plug in $M(F_{t+1}; \theta) = F_{t+1}^{-\gamma}$ with $F_{t+1} = R_{w,t+1}$, then
\[
  M_{t+1} \approx -\gamma\, \mathbb{E}_t[R_{w,t+1}]^{-\gamma-1} R_{w,t+1}
  + (\gamma + 1)\, \mathbb{E}_t[R_{w,t+1}]^{-\gamma}.
\]
Therefore,
\[
  a_t = (\gamma + 1)\, \mathbb{E}_t[R_{w,t+1}]^{-\gamma},
  \qquad
  b_t = \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_t[R_{w,t+1}]^{-\gamma-1}.
\]

\paragraph{(2) Disadvantage relative to $M_{t+1} = R_{w,t+1}^{-\gamma}$.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The original model, $M_{t+1} = R_{w,t+1}^{-\gamma}$, contains only the factor and the
    parameter, so there is no concern about additional assumptions or time-varying coefficients.
  \item After linearization, $a_t$ and $b_t$ become time-varying, so $\mathbb{E}[xR_j]$ can no
    longer be written in the form $\mathbb{E}[b_t]'\,\mathrm{Cov}(F_t, xR_{j,t})$.
  \item The time variation in $a_t$ and $b_t$ comes from $\mathbb{E}_t[R_{w,t+1}]$, as shown above.
    This conditional expectation must be estimated, and the choice of how to estimate it (e.g.,
    with a linear model or some other method) introduces estimation error. The model could be
    wrong, or there could be estimation error even if the model is correct.
  \item By imposing additional assumptions or a model on $a_t$ and $b_t$, they can be made
    time-invariant. However, if that model is wrong or the assumptions are inconsistent with
    reality, the estimates move further away from reality.
  \item $\mathbb{E}_t[\mathbf{xR}_{t+1}] = b_t'\,\mathrm{Cov}_t(F_{t+1}, \mathbf{xR}_{t+1})$ holds, but from the
    perspective of the unconditional implications used in GMM, it can be written as
    $\mathbb{E}[xR_j] = \mathbb{E}\big[b_t'\,\mathrm{Cov}_t(F_{t+1}, xR_{j,t+1})\big]$.
  \item But again, $b_t$ is determined by $\mathbb{E}_t[R_{w,t}]$. As in Problem Set 1, we already
    checked that estimating the equity premium \emph{very well}, either in sample (IS) or out of
    sample (OS), is really difficult.
\end{enumerate}
