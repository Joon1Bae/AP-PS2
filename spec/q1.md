# 1a. Do we have Complete Markets in this economy?

Yes.

**Definition (Complete Markets).** Markets are complete if there is one contingent claim
(Arrow-Debreu security) for each state of nature n = 1, ..., N, where the contingent claim for
state n pays $1 if state n materializes and $0 otherwise (Module 2, slide 1.3).

Let's define the payoff matrix X = [10, 20, 30; 10, 10, 10; 0, 10, 10]
(rows: assets A, B, C; columns: states n = 1, 2, 3).
Then, there exists \Omega s.t. X'\Omega = I, \Omega = X'^{-1} if X' is invertible.

Even if a contingent claim is not traded directly, it exists in this economy if a portfolio of
the traded assets A, B, and C replicates it, i.e., pays $1 in state n and $0 otherwise.
\Omega tells us how many units of each asset to hold to replicate the contingent claims
(column n of \Omega is the replicating portfolio for the contingent claim of state n).
Since X'\Omega = I, if X' is invertible, then a replicating portfolio for each contingent claim
exists in this economy.

Therefore, Checking whether X' is invertible or not is sufficient for Complete market in this economy.
Since det(X') = 1000 ≠ 0, X' is invertible. Therefore, this market is complete.

# 1b. Suppose that the Law of One Price holds in the economy. Calculate the price of each Contingent Claim (or Arrow-Debreu security) in this economy.

1. P(A) = \Sigma^{3}_{n=1} Pc(n) * Payoff_A(n)
2. P(B) = \Sigma^{3}_{n=1} Pc(n) * Payoff_B(n)
3. P(C) = \Sigma^{3}_{n=1} Pc(n) * Payoff_C(n)

where Pc(n) is the price of the Contingent Claim for state n.

Or, we can make it as follows:


P = X * Pc where X is the payoff matrix from 1a, Pc = [Pc(1), Pc(2), Pc(3)]', and P = [P(A), P(B), P(C)]'

We confirmed that X is invertible in problem 1a. So, we can calculate Pc = X^{-1} * P

Therefore, the prices of the Contingent Claims are

Pc = X^{-1} * P = [0, 0.1, -0.1; -0.1, 0.1, 0.2; 0.1, -0.1, -0.1] * [16, 8, 6]' = [0.2, 0.4, 0.2]',

i.e., Pc(1) = 0.2, Pc(2) = 0.4, and Pc(3) = 0.2, where
X^{-1} = [0, 0.1, -0.1; -0.1, 0.1, 0.2; 0.1, -0.1, -0.1] (det(X) = 1000).

# 1c. Suppose that the LOP holds in this economy and introduce a new asset with payoff vector Payoff(D) = [30, 20, 10] for our three states of nature (1, 2, 3). Calculate the price of asset D in this economy.

As we calculated in 1b, Pc = [0.2, 0.4, 0.2]'.
By taking inner product of Payoff(D) with Pc, we can get a price of the asset D in this economy:

P(D) = Payoff(D)' * Pc = [30, 20, 10] * [0.2, 0.4, 0.2]' = 30 * 0.2 + 20 * 0.4 + 10 * 0.2 = 16.

# 1d. Calculate the Stochastic Discount Factor (SDF) realization under each of the three states of nature in this economy. Does this economy satisfy No-Arbitrage (explain)? Is there anything interesting about the SDF in this economy (if so, explain what it means in economy terms)?

SDF => E[M * Payoff] = Price for all asset in this economy.

1. P(A) = \Sigma^{3}_{n=1} \pi(n) * M(n) * Payoff_A(n)
2. P(B) = \Sigma^{3}_{n=1} \pi(n) * M(n) * Payoff_B(n)
3. P(C) = \Sigma^{3}_{n=1} \pi(n) * M(n) * Payoff_C(n)

Or, we can make it as follows:


P = X_\pi * M where X_\pi = [10 * 1/4, 20 * 1/2, 30 * 1/4; 10 * 1/4, 10 * 1/2, 10 * 1/4; 0, 10 * 1/2, 10 * 1/4]
(X with column n multiplied by \pi(n)), M = [M(1), M(2), M(3)]', and P = [P(A), P(B), P(C)]'

Since det(X_\pi) = 31.25 ≠ 0, X_\pi is invertible, with
X_\pi^{-1} = [0, 0.4, -0.4; -0.2, 0.2, 0.4; 0.4, -0.4, -0.4].

Therefore, the realizations of the SDF are

M = X_\pi^{-1} * P = [0, 0.4, -0.4; -0.2, 0.2, 0.4; 0.4, -0.4, -0.4] * [16, 8, 6]' = [0.8, 0.8, 0.8]',

i.e., M(1) = M(2) = M(3) = 0.8.

This economy satisfies No-Arbitrage.

**Definition (No-Arbitrage).** (Module 2, slide 1.3)
- An asset with Payoff ≥ 0 must have Price ≥ 0.
- An asset with Payoff ≥ 0 and a chance for Payoff > 0 must have Price > 0.

Each Arrow-Debreu contingent claim has Payoff ≥ 0 with a chance for Payoff > 0, and from 1b
its price is positive (Pc(1) = 0.2, Pc(2) = 0.4, Pc(3) = 0.2). The contingent claims therefore
satisfy the No-Arbitrage condition above.
Moreover, with complete markets and the LOP, the price of any payoff is determined by
P = \Sigma^{3}_{n=1} Pc(n) * Payoff(n) (the formula used in 1b and 1c). Since Pc(n) > 0 for all n,
any asset with Payoff ≥ 0 has Price ≥ 0, and if its payoff is positive in at least one state,
its price is positive. Hence No-Arbitrage holds in this economy.

As we can see, the realized SDF is the same in every state of the economy (M(n) = 0.8 for all n).
Since M is constant across states, its covariance with every asset's return is zero,
Cov(M, R_j) = 0. That is, this is an economy in which the expected excess return of every asset
is zero (no risk premium).

# 1e. Show that the price that the SDF assigns to asset D in this economy is the same as the price that you calculated in Question (1c)

Using the SDF from 1d (M(n) = 0.8 for all n), with Payoff_D(1) = 30, Payoff_D(2) = 20,
and Payoff_D(3) = 10,

P(D) = \Sigma^{3}_{n=1} \pi(n) * M(n) * Payoff_D(n)
     = 0.25 * 0.8 * 30 + 0.5 * 0.8 * 20 + 0.25 * 0.8 * 10
     = 16,

which is the same as the price of asset D calculated in 1c.
